Schedule recovery: keep the baseline, the forecast and the plan distinct

When a project slips, the temptation is to redraw the programme. Real recovery starts by keeping three things separate: what was planned, what is now likely, and what it would take to recover.

3 MIN READ

A delayed project often produces a new programme that looks achievable on paper. Activities are compressed, durations shortened and the end date held. Months later the same slippage reappears, because nothing in the logic actually changed.

Three lines, not one

  • Baseline: the approved plan that performance is measured against.
  • Current forecast: an honest view of when the work will now finish, based on actual progress.
  • Recovery plan: the specific actions, resources and costs needed to close the gap.

Merging these into a single updated programme hides the size of the problem and the price of fixing it.

Test before you recommend

A recovery assessment checks whether the plan is realistic. We test the programme logic and critical path, site access, labour availability, production rates and material readiness. A recovery that depends on more workers than the site can hold, or on materials not yet ordered, is not a plan.

Acceleration always has a cost and a risk. Both are assessed and shown to the client before any recovery is recommended.

A note on contracts

Updating the programme does not change the contractual completion date. Any revision to that date follows the contract's own process and authority.

Recovery is a decision with a price, not a new bar chart.
